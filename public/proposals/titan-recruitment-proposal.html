The starting point

Twenty-five years of tribal knowledge, trapped in a remote desktop

Twenty-five seats across Perth and Queensland, around 380 engineers under contract at any one time, permanent and contract placements into the resources sector. The engine underneath is RDB: remote desktop, licenses you own outright, no vendor support left, and a full-time person keeping it alive. The decision to move to Mercury is weeks away, with go-live targeted for November, possibly February.

You were clear that Mercury is a three-year transition, not a destination. It buys robustness, the Microsoft integrations, and the LinkedIn hooks, and you will absorb the seat cost for that. What it will not give you is the layer you actually described on the call: guided workflows that pull a consultant through the process, normalized CV summaries instead of forty hours of resume scanning, interview transcripts mined for the qualitative signal that makes a placement stick. That layer you intend to build, and you want a partner who is still there after the project ships, not a vendor who does one job and disappears.

How we would look at it

Two moves, and a track that should not wait

This mirrors what we talked through. The project is two moves in sequence: the foundation, then the workflows built on it. The business-systems work Craig raised is not a later move, it runs in parallel from the start.

1
Move one  ·  the foundation: your data layer and the dashboard shell

A database you own, with the dashboard already standing on it

We never treat a third-party app as the central store. We stand up a database you own, map what lives in RDB today, and build one-way sync into it. When Mercury goes live, the sync repoints to Mercury's API and everything you have built keeps working. Your data is backed up, portable, and queryable from day one, and the "what do we do to get our data out" question in your Mercury contract stops being theoretical. We start from our company database template that covers roughly 80% of the shape, then model the last 20% around recruitment: candidates, vacancies, placements, contractors, clients. On top of it, in the same eight weeks, we deploy the dashboard shell: a Next.js/React application, browser-based login, secured, with the first views of your live data. Dave's instinct on the stack is right, and it is the same one we build on daily. This whole move is one fixed-price engagement, and it lands inside the six-to-eight-week window you set.

2
Move two  ·  guided workflows, priced per feature

Your workflow map becomes a build list we quote in tokens

You are documenting every workflow and task next week, then gap-analysing Mercury. Send us that scope of works and we return an estimate per feature in human tokens, so you choose what gets built and in what order, each one dropping into the shell from move one instead of becoming another standalone tool. The early candidates from the call: CV intake that normalizes every resume into the fifteen-line view your consultants actually want, stack-ranked per vacancy. Interview and client transcripts mined into structured, qualitative candidate and vacancy records. Advert quality checks before anything goes live. And the one Dave called the nirvana: matching that reads the soft data, the micromanager flags and the FIFO fit, not just the keywords. That is a data problem before it is an AI problem, which is why it is move two and not move one.

In parallel  ·  the business-systems track, with Francine

The low-hanging fruit that should not wait for December

Craig named it on the call: marketing running through one part-time person doing LinkedIn posts by hand, rec-support work that lands back on his desk, and no reason any of it should wait for the CRM project. We run our Four Offices of the Future workshop with Craig, Francine and the key leaders: a working session that turns the ideas in everyone's heads into a ranked roadmap across revenue, talent, operations and innovation. The marketing automations that come out of it, content pipelines, outreach, reporting, are things we have already built for other clients; what takes the work is the context behind your voice and your market, and that is exactly what the workshop captures. This track shares the same database as move one, so nothing is built twice.

Why the order

The part most people skip

Stanford looked at the successful AI programs and found around 77% of the work is not AI. It is centralising data and fixing workflows. Build the dashboard on a database you do not own and you rebuild it twice: once when RDB dies, again when the Mercury contract is up. Build it on a data layer you own and the migration underneath becomes a detail.

Craig's worry is the right one: nobody at Titan wants to spend their life maintaining tech. That is an argument for this order, not against it. Scrapers break because they chase someone else's website. A one-way sync into your own database is the boring, stable kind of plumbing, and everything downstream of it is yours to keep, not ours to ransom.

Timing

Built around your Mercury decision, not blocked by it

  • Now: you run the workflow-mapping day and finalise Mercury. We review the RDB API quality in parallel, since that decides whether the first sync reads RDB or waits for Mercury.
  • On your scope of works: we return per-feature estimates in human tokens, with a straight answer on anything we think is not worth building.
  • Weeks 1 to 8: data layer stood up, RDB mapped and synced, dashboard shell live with first views. This is the six-to-eight-week target from the call.
  • From there: workflows ship one at a time off the ranked list, and the shell simply repoints when Mercury goes live in November.
  • In parallel: the Four Offices workshop opens the business-systems track with Francine, so marketing automation does not queue behind the CRM project.
