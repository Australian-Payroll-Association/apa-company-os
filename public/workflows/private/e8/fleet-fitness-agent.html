Fleet Fitness Agent

The equipment register already knows every machine we own, and who holds it. It just cannot tell us which ones can still do the work. This agent reads the register on a schedule, cross-references each laptop against its holder's role, grades the engineer machines against a hardware policy, and flags what to upgrade, redistribute, or stop buying, before a starved machine slows an engineer down.

Why

We have an inventory, not a monitor

The company_os.equipment register holds 23 active items: 21 laptops and 2 monitors, with assignments and requests already wired to the admin. That is a good record of what we own. What it cannot do is tell us whether a machine is still fit for the job, for the person actually using it. Nobody is watching the gap between the spec on file and the spec the work needs.

Only engineers are in scope right now. Cross-referencing every laptop against its holder's role narrows the fleet to 12 engineer-held machines, and the picture there is sharp:

  • 5 of 12 engineer laptops sit below the app-dev floor of 24 GB RAM and 512 GB SSD. Every one is RAM-bound at 16 to 18 GB; their disks are fine.
  • AI Engineers are the hardest hit. Three of the four are on 16 GB. If AI work is the priority, this is the priority.
  • The newest purchase went to an engineer, under-spec. The July 2026 M5 was assigned to an AI Engineer at 16 GB. The under-spec buying is ongoing, and it is landing on the people in scope.
  • Redistribution barely helps here. The one capable spare is a 64 GB Windows ThinkPad, and the failing engineers are mostly on Macs. This is a buy-RAM problem, not a shuffle-the-cupboard one.

The role filter is not a nicety, it changes the answer. A fleet-wide pass flagged a 256 GB machine as the sharpest edge; once graded against role, that machine turned out to belong to a recruiter and dropped out of scope entirely. Grading without knowing who holds the machine points the budget at the wrong people.

The cost is silent. A starved machine does not fail loudly. It swaps, throttles, and quietly taxes the engineer on it every day. By the time anyone raises it, the machine is already 18 months into a life it will not finish. A weekly agent turns that silent tax into a visible list.

The model

Cross-reference the holder, grade the in-scope machines, act on the outliers

The agent does exactly what the manual review did this week, on a schedule, and never forgets to run. It joins every laptop to its holder's role first, so grading runs only on machines held by people in scope. Today that scope is engineers. Each in-scope laptop then gets one of three grades against policy:

PASS
Meets the floor
At or above 24 GB / 512 GB and under the replacement age. No action.
WATCH
At the edge
Exactly at the floor, low free disk, or nearing the 4-year mark. Plan ahead.
FAIL
Below the floor
Under 24 GB RAM or 512 GB SSD. On the upgrade list.
The policy the agent grades against App development, no local model work. Floor: 24 GB RAM, 512 GB SSD. Preferred: 48 GB, 1 TB. Keep at least 15 percent of the disk free. Flag any laptop over 4 years old for the replacement cycle.

How a run flows

Register in, action list out

  1. Read the register. The agent pulls every active row from company_os.equipment.
  2. Cross-reference the holder. Each machine is joined to its holder's role via current_team_members. Engineer-held laptops are in scope; everyone else is listed but set aside.
  3. Normalize the spec. RAM, storage, CPU, and model year are parsed into clean numbers, even from messy invoice-string fields. Rows it cannot parse go to the data-gap list.
  4. Grade against policy. Each in-scope laptop is scored PASS, WATCH, or FAIL, with the failing dimension named.
  5. Rank and match. FAIL machines are ordered by severity, then matched against in-stock units that would fix them.
  6. Report. The fleet page updates, and a short digest lands each quarter: new failures, resolved ones, redistribution moves, and any below-floor purchase caught since the last run.

The register stays the source of truth. The agent never invents a machine or edits a spec on its own. It reads what admin holds, grades it, and writes back only its verdict. Humans still make every buy, upgrade, and reassignment call.

Build plan

Three PRs on the register we already have

The equipment schema, the admin CRUD at /admin/operations/equipment, and the team request form all exist. This work adds grading and reporting on top. No new destructive migrations.

PR 1

Holder role join, spec normalization, and grading

WHAT IT DOES

Joins each laptop to its holder's role via current_team_members and marks engineer-held machines as in scope. Parses the messy processor, ram, and storage fields into clean structured values, and adds a computed fitness grade per in-scope laptop against the policy. Backfills the one blank machine (EQ-0014) and surfaces any it cannot parse.

DONE WHEN

Every engineer laptop has a clean RAM, SSD, and grade, the 5 known below-floor engineer machines all read FAIL, non-engineer holders are listed as out of scope, and the data-gap list names anything unparseable.

PR 2

Fleet fitness page and quarterly digest

WHAT IT DOES

Adds a fitness view to the equipment admin: grades, the ranked upgrade list, redistribution suggestions, and the data-gap list. A quarterly routine runs the grading and writes the digest (to file, and to Lark if configured).

DONE WHEN

The page renders today's engineer fleet with correct grades, and the first digest leads with the 5 below-floor engineer machines (3 of them AI Engineers), names EQ-0018 as the one capable spare, and notes it is Windows so a Mac engineer would need a buy instead.

PR 3

Purchase guard

WHAT IT DOES

When a machine is added or edited below the floor, the register flags it against the policy right there in the form, and the next digest calls it out. Stops the under-spec buying at the point of entry.

DONE WHEN

Adding a 16 GB laptop shows a policy warning on save, and that machine appears in the digest's caught-purchases section.

After PR 2, this week's manual review becomes a page that refreshes itself, and the agent's first real job is simply to keep it honest.

Phase 2

From static specs to live health Planned

Everything above grades the spec on file. It does not yet see how a machine behaves under real load. Phase 2 adds that, and it is confirmed as the next step: a lightweight on-device reporter on each engineer's Mac sends a small health snapshot on a schedule, turning the register into a live monitor. Macs first; Windows machines follow once the Mac reporter is proven.

  • Memory pressure and swap, the single best signal that a machine is under-spec for its actual workload.
  • Free disk percentage, warning under 15 percent.
  • Thermal pressure under sustained load.
  • OS and toolchain drift, so environments stay reproducible.

Phase 2 is out of scope for v1, not off the table. On-device reporting means installing a small agent on every engineer Mac. v1 delivers value from the data we already hold; Phase 2 turns that snapshot into a live feed once the grading foundation is in place.
